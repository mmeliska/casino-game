## Summary

The story is an open-ended life sim: a blank-slate protagonist arrives in a big gambling city, and the town's NPCs carry the story, shaping your path while you shape theirs.

**Decided**

- No hard deadline or story clock as the main driver; players can play casually and take their time.
- Life-simulator feel rather than a timed run.
- Blank-slate protagonist: the player is the protagonist.
- NPCs have their own stories that affect your advancement, and you can affect theirs.
- Consequences can surface much later, in a chance meeting with an NPC whose life changed because of you.
- Some NPCs also change because the story is written that way, independent of the player. The Starflare's fate is the background story, with several possible outcomes; most play time goes to exploring the city, moving up and down the casino ladder, and uncovering NPC stories and a mystery or two.
- One big fictional Great Lakes city as home base, split by a river that forms a state line, with occasional excursions out of town. Midwestern, not desert, so it doesn't read as another Las Vegas.

**Proposed, not yet confirmed**

- "New in Town" as the framing premise: you arrive in the city with about $200.
- The draft cast below (Rosa, Dutch, Teddy, Vivian, Gus).
- Seeds and reveals as the mechanism for delayed consequences.

## Structure: an open-ended life sim

There is no timer on the story; main beats unlock from milestones and relationships, not days elapsed. A hard deadline would make going broke feel like losing, which works against the design pillar that broke is content.

Reference points: Stardew Valley (no fail state, a soft goal pursued at your own pace, story through townspeople) and BitLife (random life events that branch on your circumstances).

- **Soft spine.** The Starflare's fate runs in the background while players explore at their own pace.
- **Time is flavor.** Days and seasons drive time-of-day events, NPC schedules and holidays. Short local timers still fit in places, such as a loan coming due or an invitation that expires, but nothing ends the game.
- **A home that tracks your life.** For example: sleeping in your car, a weekly motel, an apartment, a suite. A visible measure of how you're doing beyond the bankroll number.
- **Endings.** The main story resolves and play continues afterward, Stardew-style. The ending centers on what became of the Starflare, which depends on your state and choices. A possible "cash out" or retirement option lets the player choose when their story ends, with an epilogue shaped by their state.

## Protagonist: a blank slate

The player is the protagonist, so the game never tells them who they are; the town defines itself, and the player defines the protagonist through choices.

- **No dictated backstory.** You arrive in town with a little cash and no explanation. Option: let the player pick a one-line reason at the start (a fresh start, a hunch, nowhere else to go) that only tints a few early lines, or never ask.
- **Choices build a reputation, not a personality.** NPCs react to what you've done: paying debts back or stiffing people, sharing winnings, taking sketchy jobs. This can be a few hidden traits (generous, reliable, ruthless) that events check.
- **Action-first dialogue.** Choices are things to do or short neutral replies, not lines with a strong personality.

## Setting: a big city with excursions

The game is set in a big fictional Great Lakes city, Chicago-like rather than Vegas-like, where a river flows into the lake and the river is the state line. One side legalized gambling, at first only on the water, so the oldest casinos are boats. Later the law loosened and land casinos rose along the lakefront. The other side of the river stayed dry.

The backdrop: machine politics, an old mob history, union power, and old money versus new money.

- **One home city.** Chance meetings, a home base and a recurring cast all depend on staying in one place. It also keeps art and content scope down.
- **Neighborhood-scale community.** You live in one part of a big city, and the recurring faces are the people in your corner of it.
- **Seasons that matter.** Winter blizzards empty the streets and keep people at the tables; summer brings lakefront festivals; the lake freezes and the yacht games stop until spring.
- **Themed casinos.** Every casino has a theme, which gives each venue an instant look at pixel scale. Examples: the Starflare's rockets, a paddlewheel riverboat, a lighthouse hotel.
- **Excursions.** Short, milestone-unlocked trips, each a setting for a story arc or special game. Ideas: a riverboat trip downstate, a lake resort up north in summer, a harness racing track in farm country, a high-stakes game in a hunting lodge.

**The venue ladder is the map.** Moving up in the world means moving toward the lake; going broke means crossing the bridge.

| District | Character | Venues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Across the river (dry side) | Steel mills, rail yards, corner taverns, union halls; cheap to live, high heat; Dutch's territory | Street dice, back-room card games, a numbers racket, bookies, pawn shops, the weekly motel |
| The bridges | Drawbridges that lift for boat traffic and strand everyone waiting, a natural spot for chance meetings | Gas station slots at the foot of the main bridge |
| River district (old downtown) | The original casino district, faded but storied | Old riverboat casinos, some docked and rusting; early land casinos; the Starflare Club |
| Lakefront strip | Tall towers along the shore with an elevated train running past | Hotel casinos |
| Gold coast | Old money and the newest wealth | Fancy resorts, private clubs in lakefront mansions, yacht games on the lake |

## The Starflare: the background story

The Starflare's fate is the game's overarching story, but it stays in the background: most play time goes to exploring the city, working up and down the casino ladder, and uncovering NPC stories and mysteries. Its beats surface occasionally, triggered by milestones and choices.

The Starflare Club, "the Flare" to locals, is one of the first land casinos, built in the 1960s at the height of the space race, with a giant rocket sign and a planetarium lounge. By the time you arrive it is a run-down river district casino and an early hangout you grow attached to: Gus, the regulars, the rocket sign. Partway through the story it runs into trouble, and what happens next depends on you.

| Outcome | Likely path there |
| --- | --- |
| Rebuilt on the lakefront as a grand, over-the-top luxury casino | The richest players; strong relationships with the cast |
| Bought out with a small renovation, staying in the river district | Comfortable bankroll; a partnership with Gus |
| Closes for good | You couldn't or didn't step in |
| Taken over by organized crime | High heat, debts or deals with the wrong people |
| Condemned | Neglect; nobody with money cares enough |
| Burns down | The worst case, possibly tied to a mystery: accident, insurance job or message |

The lakefront rebuild, renamed simply the Starflare, is the 1960s dream of the future finally built. Possible phases: buy the name and sign; move and relight the rocket; the gaming floor; a tower with space-station suites and an observation deck; a rooftop "launch pad" high-roller room. After it opens, owner mode (hosting whales, poaching dealers, rival casinos) can carry post-story play.

Who you bring in (Gus, Vivian, Rosa, Dutch, Teddy) shapes each outcome, and the regulars scattering across the city when the Starflare falls into trouble is natural material for later reveals.

Considered and set aside: a small town, which can't credibly hold high-end resorts; a multi-city tour, which turns the life sim into a journey and scatters the cast; and a desert setting, which reads too much like Las Vegas. Earlier premises set aside: The Wager, Hot Money and Last Week of the Starlight (built on hard deadlines), and The Inheritance and The Comeback (defined protagonists).

## NPCs with two-way stories

NPCs carry most of the story, and influence runs both ways: their fortunes affect your advancement, and your choices change their lives.

Each key NPC has a small state of their own, so the money gate applies to them too. You can be the one who bails someone out, or the reason they go under.

- **Fortune:** a coarse level (broke, getting by, flush), nudged by events and story beats rather than simulated hand by hand.
- **Arc stage:** where they are in their own story.
- **Standing with you:** how they see you, informed by your reputation.

Arcs should cross, so helping one person can cost or help another.

**Draft cast**

| Character | Where you meet them | Their story | How you can change it | How it changes yours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rosa | Gas station | Former dealer, blacklisted from the big casinos | Dig into the blacklisting and clear her name | Teaches poker tells and how to spot rigged games; later deals at the hotel casino, a friend on the inside |
| Dutch | Street and run-down casino | Loan shark | Be his client, run collections for him, or bring him down | Loans and heat-raising jobs; if he falls, someone worse may take his corner |
| Teddy | Arrives when you do | The other newcomer climbing the same ladder | Stake him when he's broke, or leave him to sink | Ally who brings you into games, or a rival who sells you out |
| Vivian | Hotel and fancy casino | High roller on her own losing streak | Help her before she loses everything, or not | Treats you as a lucky charm and opens rooms you can't afford, until your luck turns |
| Gus | The Starflare Club | Owner of the struggling casino | Stand by him before and after the Starflare closes | His casino closes partway through; whether he's part of its reopening depends on your relationship |

Crossing examples: clearing Rosa's name may cost Dutch a source of income; staking Teddy may put you in Dutch's debt; Vivian's fortune may decide whether Gus gets an investor.

## Seeds, reveals and authored drift

Players discover their impact rather than being told: a small early moment pays off much later in a chance meeting with someone whose life changed.

**Seeds** are small, forgettable moments that quietly set a flag. Examples: tossing $20 to a busker outside the run-down casino; snitching on a card counter to get on the pit boss's good side; taking a courier job without asking what's in the bag.

**Reveals** are later random events that check those flags. Examples: weeks later at the hotel casino, the busker, now headlining a lounge act, buys you a drink; the card counter you burned turns up running the private game you were just invited to, and the invitation was a setup.

Guidelines for reveals:

- **Let time pass.** Gate them with something like `days_since(seed) > 14` and give them low weight, so they feel like chance.
- **Show them anywhere.** Spread reveals across venues so they catch the player off guard.
- **Give a callback cue.** One line that clicks the memory into place ("You don't remember me, do you?").
- **Let some seeds go nowhere.** Players should never learn which moments matter.

**Authored drift.** Some NPCs change because the story says so: Teddy hits a hot streak, Vivian sells her house, Dutch gets a new partner. The player's flags decide the flavor of the change, not whether it happens. Teddy's streak happens either way; whether he's grateful or out to show you up depends on what you did when he was broke.

**Engine fit.** This needs almost no schema change: a seed is an event whose effects set a flag, and a reveal is an event whose conditions require that flag plus elapsed time. NPC arcs are event chains whose conditions check both your state and the NPC's, for example `bankroll > 2000 AND teddy.fortune == "broke" AND teddy.stage == 2`.
