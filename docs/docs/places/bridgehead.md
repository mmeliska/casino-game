## About

A gas station at the main bridge: worst odds in town, best gossip. Mitch is at the counter, and his race book is out back. The machines and scratchers are licensed; the race book isn't (Mike, 2026-11-06). Its video poker is Jacks or Better on a 6/5 table (a full house pays 6, a flush 5), the worst pay in town, printed on the glass (Mike, 2026-11-07).

**Mitch's back room.** His Haybale Downs race book (events, not a game). It opens up as your straight/crooked scale allows. The bad tip that ruined him came from Bennett (R5).

**The slots** (Mike, 2026-11-22): a cheap three-reel called Triple Bar, plain and old, no theme: cherries, single, double and triple BAR, and a red 7, with a blank between every symbol. Three rows show in the window and the one payline runs across the middle. $1 a credit, 1 to 3 credits. It pays back 86% at one credit and 88% at three, the worst machine in town (the 6/5 poker beside it is about 95%); it pays something about one pull in four and a half, and three 7s come up once in 10,648 pulls. The glass, per credit:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7 7 7 | 200 | 400 | 1,200 |
| Triple bar x3 | 80 | 160 | 240 |
| Double bar x3 | 40 | 80 | 120 |

| Cherry x2 | 10 | 20 | 30 |
| Any bar x3 | 5 | 10 | 15 |
| Cherry | 2 | 4 | 6 |

Cherries count from the left reel. Every stop on a reel is equally likely and the strip you see is the strip that spins, so the odds are what the reels say. Bulbs run round the reel window: a slow chase while it waits, a fast one while the reels turn, all of them flashing on a win, and the halves against each other for three 7s.

**The scratchers** (Mike, 2026-11-23): a roll of ten behind the counter, $1 to $5, bought and scratched with a finger at the counter ("Buy scratchers"). The names, looks and prize pools are proposals, to tune in playtesting. Each ticket is printed before it's sold, as a real roll is: its prize comes off the pool first, then its face is printed to show that prize and nothing more, so every loser really loses and every winner wins once. Scratching only shows it, and you can't walk off with a ticket half scratched. A spot falls away once more than half its latex is off, and "Scratch it all" takes the lot.

They're the worst odds in town, the way a lottery's are: the $1 tickets give back 57 to 60 cents on the dollar, the $5 ones about 68, and about one ticket in four wins something, most often its price back. Top prizes are long shots (1 in 100,000 to 1 in 333,333).

Every ticket has art (Mike, 2026-11-23): a picture across the top and flat colour below, drawn as an upright card and a landscape one. The game prints on the flat colour and never over the picture: the name, how to play, the play area under silver latex, and the odds and a ticket number in small print at the foot, with the price in a badge in the top corner. Upright, they stack down the card. On a phone held sideways the game deals the landscape card instead, with the name, how to play and the odds on the left and the play area on the right. Turn the phone mid-ticket and it's the same ticket on the other card: spots you've read stay read, and a spot only part scratched comes back covered. On the rack each tile shows its ticket's picture with the name below it. A ticket without art is printed in its own colours. Mitch has a word when you step away; his lines are proposals.

### The scratcher symbols

The pictures under the latex, so a spot reads like a real ticket's (Mike, 2026-11-23): Gold Rush's finds and Lucky 7s' slot symbols each have a picture, with the word kept small underneath as a caption; Ice Fishing's and Photo Finish's numbers each get their own fish or horse above them; and the tickets whose spots are only amounts or numbers get one faint picture behind every spot (Penny Jar's penny). Until a picture exists the spot prints its word or number alone.
