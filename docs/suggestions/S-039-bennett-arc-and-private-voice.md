## Summary
State what Bennett wants (the Levee to be a place his father wouldn't have lost a house in) and needs (to stop making his father's shame into policy), map how the three act 4 routes land on him, and give the private voice four lines.

## Current state
Bennett: the Levee's alderman and ward boss; loves the Levee; publicly against gambling, takes bribes; his father lost his house to Flare markers; runs for mayor on "clean up Northlight" with Starflare Field; "an obstacle you negotiate with, never defeat"; keeps the wide-angle tape as insurance; his stance at the hearing is "set by your act 4 route" (people/bennett.md). Two voices are named; only the public one has lines.

## Proposed change
**Want:** a Levee with a dome over it and no tables in it, so no old man can ever again sign his house away at a felt. Everything he says in public is this want in a suit.

**Need:** to stop punishing a building for what his father did at it. The Flare didn't take Richard's house; Richard did, and Bennett has built a stadium rather than say so.

**Contradiction:** he keeps the Levee's light, not its dice, and takes the dice's money to do it. He knows. The campaign slogan is the lie he tells with speeches; the envelope at Dot's desk is the truth he tells with cash.

**Arc by route:**
- **Room 9, reconcile:** they play a hand of War, his father hands him the box, and the amendment is gratitude. At the hearing he speaks for the new Starflare, briefly, and says the word "casino" without flinching. His mayor's race headline is a win.
- **Room 9, shame:** he amends under threat; at the hearing he's silent and hostile; the headline is a loss by a few points, and the epilogue's newspaper has him back as alderman, bitter, still paying the room.
- **By the book (A4.11):** the commission voids the name clause; he is embarrassed in public for the first time in his career and, oddly, relieved. Headline: a narrow loss and a line about "a conflict of interest." He sends Richard's envelope with a note for the first time.
- **Expose Umbra (A4.3):** his cut comes out with Wren's books. He is the only person in the game who can go to prison, and whether he does is the epilogue's front page. If the player handed Ed the clean logs only, he survives, weakened.
- **Badly for the player:** if you signed his petition (S7) and fought dirty at A3.2, he reads your name at the council hearing with Gus in the room (as S7 already does), and at the license hearing (A5.7) his hostility sets the strictest conditions the game offers. He never raises his voice.

**Private voice, sample lines** (the second and fourth break his "never mentions his father" rule, once each, in private; the public rule holds everywhere else):
- (At the car window) "Go to bed."
- (To Gus, after the reconcile fires) "He let me win. He always did. You knew that."
- (To the player, by-the-book route, after the ruling) "You did it the right way. I'd have respected the other one more. That's the problem with me."
- (The last envelope note, insert) "Dad. It's Tuesday. B."

## Why it works
A negotiated antagonist needs an inner life the player can bargain against, and a need the routes can satisfy or deny. Mapping the routes onto his ending makes the player's act 4 choice visible on the epilogue's front page, which is already where the docs put the mayor's race.

## How the player experiences it
Through the hearing's tone, the newspaper panel, and the four private lines.

## Risks and tradeoffs
"Can go to prison" is a stronger consequence than the docs currently give any character; it fits the expose route's ruthless black-hat version and should be gated to it. The by-the-book line gives Bennett a flash of self-knowledge; keep it to once.
