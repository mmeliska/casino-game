## Summary
The arrogant newcomer who falls is the game's most trope-shaped character. Subvert it without changing the arc: the rival plays straight all game, falls on fair tables and bad luck alone, and their showiness is the voice of someone who has already lost one house and can't say so.

## Current state
Chase/Brooke: white, 30s, upper-middle-income family, "divorced, no kids, starting fresh," a head start on you; "starts friendly and turns arrogant and self-centered as they succeed"; luck turns, borrows from Ray, asks you for cash; "help them and all is good; refuse and they may end up stealing from you" (people/chase.md). Voice: "their wins, never their losses; lies by overstating." Tell (proposal): checks their phone, adjusts a watch or cuff. Nothing says why they're in Northlight or what the divorce cost.

## Proposed change
**Wound:** the divorce took the house. Their ex got it, and the rival got the car, the watch and a settlement that is the head start. "Starting fresh" is what you call it when you can't say "I lost the house." In a game where Richard lost his house to the Flare and Gus's whole life is his father's house, the rival is the only one who lost theirs to something as ordinary as a marriage, and the only one who won't admit a loss happened.

**Want:** to win back a house-sized number before anyone notices they lost one. **Need:** to say the word "lost" out loud.

**Subversion:** the rival plays clean. No crooked jobs, no Cinder Room, never a day's work for Umbra; the loan from Ray is the only contact, and it's a loan, not a job. Their fall is the fair-tables rule made flesh: a careful, honest, well-funded player who loses to variance and then to Ray's interest, which is the only unfair thing that touches them. The player, who may be climbing dirty, watches the clean player sink. When the rival asks for cash at A3.8, the ask is honest for the first time: "I'm not up. I haven't been up since spring." Overstating, dropped.

**How it shows:**
- **The watch.** The adjusted-cuff tell is them checking a watch that's the last thing from the old life. If they ever pawn it at Troy's (S-031), the tell disappears from their poses.
- **The house word.** They never say "house" except as the casino. Once, if the player helps at A3.8, they say "my house" about the ex's, and correct it.
- **Ed leaves them alone.** The one newcomer with zero heat. If the player has heat, Ed can say so: "Your friend's clean. I checked."
- **The theft (A4.8, S-031) is the exception that proves it.** The first crooked thing they do in Northlight is against you, and it isn't a job, it's a pawn ticket. A clean player's one crime is the measure of how far they fell.
- **The mirror line (act 4, A4.8 "all is well" branch):** "You know what the difference between us was? You picked a side of the river. I just kept crossing."

## Why it works
A rival who is a worse person than the player is a trope; a rival who is a better gambler and a more honest one, and loses anyway, is a mirror and a warning. It makes the fair-play pillar a character beat, and it makes refusing them at A3.8 cost something: you're refusing the one person who did it right. Their wound is a lost house too, from the side nobody expects.

## How the player experiences it
Nothing structural changes. The lines change, the watch insert gains meaning, and Ed gets one line. The player notices, late, that the rival never showed up anywhere crooked until the theft.

## Risks and tradeoffs
It removes the option of the rival as a crooked foil, which no doc uses anyway. "Divorced, no kids" is settled and kept; the house is new. If Mike wants the rival to lean crooked in some branch, keep the clean rule for acts 1 to 3 only.
