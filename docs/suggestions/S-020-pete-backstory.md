## Summary
A 350-word history of Petras, the Flare's pit boss, written to the settled facts.

## Current state
Lithuanian-American, about 60, grew up in the Levee alongside Gus; mill family; hired at 19 by the founder to bus tables at the supper club; one of the first dealers when the license came; best man at Gus's wedding; "Uncle" to Stella; kept the Flare running after the funeral; the gold 25-year rocket pin; "Tae keeps trying to hire him away"; arranged room 9 at Dot's motel for Gus, so that Dot only knows Pete asked a favor (people/pete.md).

## Proposed change
Petras was the third son of a mill family in the Levee, back when the Levee was mill families; his grandfather came over for the furnaces and his father kept the same job until the furnaces went cold. He was supposed to follow. At nineteen he walked into the Starflare supper club in his brother's suit because the sign was the brightest thing on the river, and Mr. Hamilton, who could read a face, gave him a bus tray and a rule: count everything, say nothing. He has kept the rule for forty-one years.

He learned cards the way a busboy does, standing behind the game, and when the license came and the supper club became a casino he was one of the first men Mr. Hamilton handed a shoe. He dealt for years and has run the pit for decades, and in all that time the only person he ever lost a job for was himself, once, when he told Mr. Hamilton the band was being paid too much, and Mr. Hamilton, at Pete's twenty-fifth year and a few months before his own death, gave him the pin with a note that said only "You were right about the band." He has never worked anywhere else and has never wanted to, which is not the same as never being offered.

He was Gus's older brother in everything but blood: he taught him to count a rack, stood up at his wedding, and held Stella at the cage while Gus's wife counted the drop. When Alice died he did what the rule said: counted everything, said nothing, and kept the Flare running while Gus couldn't. Years before, the year he got his first shoe, he had married Ona, a school-bus driver from two streets over. They have two kids: a grown daughter, and the youngest, Tomas, still at college, and the pit boss's pay hasn't moved in years. Ona has never set foot on the floor, by agreement: the Flare gets his nights and she gets his mornings. Stella grew up calling him Uncle because his kids called Gus the same.

He knows about the payroll night, and he knows the room 9 arrangement because he made it; the Cinder Room he suspects and has never been told (you can tell him, A1.9), and the cameras and the key he knows nothing of. He is the only one who tells Gus hard truths, and he has told him every one that can be said about the floor, the numbers and the band; the ones about the things Gus won't name, he keeps, because Gus has never asked and Pete's rule is to count them and say nothing. That is the cost of him: he is loyal past the point where loyalty helps. Tae's offer is the first time in forty years the arithmetic has beaten the rule (Tomas's tuition against a pit boss's pay), and he hands you the pin because the one hard truth he can't tell Gus is this one.

## Why it works
It makes the rule ("count everything, say nothing") the engine of his voice sheet and his silence in every mystery, and it frames his leaving as the rule finally failing rather than a job change. It keeps the founder offstage with a single gesture (the pin as an apology), which fits the docs' "no plot role."

## How the player experiences it
Only through the pin, the card, the rule said once, early, while you're still "kid" ("Count everything, kid. Say nothing. Worked for forty years."), and Stella calling him Uncle.

## Risks and tradeoffs
Minor new facts: third son, the brother's suit, and a family (wife Ona, a grown daughter, and the youngest, Tomas, at college). The family is offstage and has no portraits. Its one job is to explain "can't afford to retire yet." Names need a clash check. The founder's "count everything, say nothing" line characterizes Mr. Hamilton slightly; it is offered as Pete's rule, which he attributes to the old man.
