## Summary
A 350-word history of Vivian consistent with the bible and S-033, and five lines in her voice.

## Current state
Black old money; her great-grandfather founded an insurance company that covered the mill and dock workers nobody else would insure; fills the Silvershore family head role; early 60s, silver bob, pearls, camel coat, sunglasses indoors; dry wit, old-money understatement (people/vivian.md).

## Proposed change
**Backstory.** The family money is a hundred years old and started as a kindness with a premium: her great-grandfather wrote policies for furnace men and dock crews the lakefront firms wouldn't touch, collected door to door in Backwater and the Levee, and paid out when the others found reasons not to. By her grandfather's time the company owned half of Silvershore and the parcel at the line, and he was the kind of rich man who still ate at the Flare because the Flare was where the men he'd insured went to feel rich for a night. He took his granddaughter, not his sons, to the tables, because she could sit still and count. She was fourteen the night he shook Hamilton's hand (S-033).

Her father and uncles were a different generation: they sold the company to a national firm, kept the land, moved the family's name onto a foundation and off the policies. Vivian married a man from the Mile who liked that she was old money and left when he found out what it cost to keep. No children; a nephew in another state who sends a card. She has run the family's affairs for thirty years, which means she has watched it become smaller and more respectable every one of them, and she decided at some point, without announcing it, that she'd rather it ended at a table than in a trust.

She plays because the tables are the last place in Northlight where her family's money does what it was for: it goes to the house, and the house pays the dealers, and the dealers are the grandchildren of the men on the policies. She is very good at losing because she has made it a discipline. She wears copies of her pearls and says nothing because the pearls are a family story she's not required to finish for anyone. She has watched the Flare decline from across the water with the only guilt she has ever admitted to, privately, which is that her family kept a promise's land and not the promise.

**Sample dialogue.**
- "Darling, I've been losing since before you were born. I'm very good at it."
- On the yacht: "You can see the whole city from here. I prefer the part that flickers."
- At the Gilded Pine: "A man used to bring his boy to my grandfather's table. The man ran a supper club. A rough season, that was, after." (She has changed the subject before the player can ask.)
- At the jeweler, the drive job: "If anyone asks, we went to lunch. We did, after all."
- A5.1, sunglasses off: "This one I'd like to win."

## Why it works
It gives her losses a logic that is neither pathology nor plot convenience: a discipline, with a destination. The insurance history already in the docs becomes the reason the land matters to her, and it ties her to Backwater and the Levee through the men on the policies, which is the ensemble link she's missing.

## How the player experiences it
Lines only. The policies can appear once as an insert: a framed first policy on the Obsidian's wall, in her great-grandfather's hand, for a furnace man (no name shown).
