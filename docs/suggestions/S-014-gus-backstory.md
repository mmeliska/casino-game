## Summary
A 400-word history of Gus for the writers' reference, consistent with the bible and with S-011 to S-013. Nothing here is meant to be spoken in the game; it is what the lines are written against.

## Current state
Gus's page gives the facts (founder's son, late 50s, wife was Dot's sister, the payroll night, the camera deal, the Cinder Room, the box for Richard) as a list (people/gus.md). There is no narrative of how he became the man at the door.

## Proposed change
Gus was born in the Flare, nearly: his mother went into labor at a supper-club table and Mr. Hamilton finished the set before he drove her. He grew up under the planetarium ceiling, doing homework at the bar while Pete, three years older, from the block, and already busing, taught him to count a rack by eye. He was the owner's boy, which meant everyone was kind and nobody was straight with him, and he learned to fill a silence with a story about the old days before he was old enough to have any.

When the license came and the supper club became a casino, his father ran the floor and Gus ran the room: comps, birthdays, the band's bar tab. He married Alice the winter the piano arrived; she picked it, and repainted the ceiling, and told him he was a host, not a gambler, and should stay one. His father died when Stella was ten and left him the Flare, the jacket and a stack of markers nobody had ever called in, because Mr. Hamilton had believed a debt between friends wasn't one.

He started going under the Stacks after a bad year, telling himself it was business, keeping an ear open for the Levee. Casino war suited him: no choices, no bluffing, nothing to be good at. The night Alice died he had his phone in his coat (S-012). He has told himself since that the Flare is for her, and the habit is for nothing, and that a man who never says the word "debt" doesn't have one.

Then the payroll night: drunk, a shortfall, Rosa dealing him back to even without being asked, and a camera that saw her hands and not his face. He kept the wide-angle tape. He let her lose her license. He has not said her name since. The commission ordered cameras he couldn't afford; a young woman with a tablet offered him some; when he said no, her people showed him himself at a war table at 3am. He signed knowing. Bennett found out and made him clear an old man's markers; Gus gave the old man a key, because it was the closest he could get to an apology without making one.

By the time you arrive he is the kindest man in the Levee and has not finished a sentence in years. Every true thing about him is held by someone else: the tape by Bennett, the footage by Wren, the key by Richard, the name by Dot, the number by Stella. The Flare is a museum of other people's things and he is the docent. He gives you coffee because it is the only thing in the building that's his to give.

## Why it works
Backstory written as a shape (a boy who filled silences, a man whose truths are all held by others) gives writers a through-line for every scene, and it makes the act 4 and 5 beats land as the moment the things start coming back to him. It reconciles the existing facts into a single wound rather than a list of secrets.

## How the player experiences it
Never directly. In fragments: Pete's rack-counting line, Stella's "homework at the bar" story, the mug, the ceiling, the key.

## Risks and tradeoffs
"Mr. Hamilton believed a debt between friends wasn't one" lightly characterizes the founder, who the docs keep offstage with no plot role; it's flavour and can be cut. Everything else is consistent with the bible.
